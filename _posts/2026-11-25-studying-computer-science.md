---
title: Studying Computer Science in the AI era and why it's still worth doing.
topic: Education
---
This autumn I started studying Computer Science at the Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm, having found my previous life working in M&A and Private Equity monotonous at its best and self-destructive at its worst. 

Few are presented with the opportunity to start all over again, being constrained by relational obligations, the prospect of even more student debt, missing out on income earning years, or simply the dread of having to face several years of late-night deadlines and dry academic books.

My reflections on why I chose to go back to university is however the topic of another post. A lot of thought went into my choice of CS, and specifically KTH, and it is in the backdrop of the AI revolution, with the loudest voices chanting about the "advent of AGI" and the "total obliteration of white-collar jobs - starting with the software engineers" that I wish to elaborate on why I deliberately made this choice.

Open up LinkedIn, X, or any of the large social-media platforms that don't rely on short form content (At least not entirely) and you'll tend to find three camps regarding software engineers: In one you have the AI optimists who have given themselves completely to the new paradigm. Posts about spinning up 500 agents to "one-shot" the whole code base, how their 14-step prompt framework will turn Claude into a McKinsey consultant, analogies towards the invention of the printing press, and presentation of their 47th internally built tool to automate even the most minor workflow are so prevalent that one might think these posts themselves form part of their workflows. In the second camp you have the AI pessimists, these often consist of disillusioned seasoned engineers lamenting the end of "proper" coding since AI will do it both better and faster, as well as embittered early-stage engineers who face one of the harshest entry-level job markets in recent times. Posts about switching careers entirely, a profound loss of purpose, and nostalgic longing for the old order form the entirety of their corpus to the overall discussion. In the third camp you have people who don't belong to either camp but simply "shitpost", often this takes the form of "CS and software engineering is beyond cooked" with some reference to either Claude or ChatGPT. 

As divergent as the camps may seem, a common line amongst all seems to be that computer science is a field is essentially dead. Among the optimists it's dead because AI knows the entirety of the field better than you and you should focus on utilizing AI to the maximum extent for other ventures, among the pessimists it's dead because there won't be a job market for it and the role has fundamentally morphed into something foreign, and among the "shitposters" it's dead because CS is just outputting code which AI can and will do better than you.

I think all of these camps hold a myopic view of the purpose of education. It's reminiscent of that line everyone heard from someone in elementary school about "Why should we learn this when we're never gonna use it in the real world? We should learn how to do taxes instead or how to get a job". The answer to the previous, which is next to impossible to imprint on someone that young, is that education shapes the way you think rather than imparting immediate practical skills. Long before the "solve everything with AI" button came to universities, students would have the same routine: You went to classes, you did the exercises, wrote essays, did hand-ins, and eventually you would have some form of exam at the end. Every exercise, every essay, every lab and every exam you do during those three to five years contributes absolutely nothing to the advancement of any field or the production of anything new and novel. Those who critique the need for universities today due to AI seem to be responding to this exact procedure and missing the point entirely. Nothing you learn in university is really exclusive domain, you could have made the same argument in 2001 that the infinite amount of knowledge available on the World Wide Web will negate any need for formal education since people can just "learn anything online". The point here is that if you take a class in linear algebra you will be doing by hand what is exclusively done by a computer in "real life". The same applies to a class in programming, differential equations, operating systems, or any other technical discipline. Nothing has fundamentally changed this procedure, checking the answer in the back of the book or asking Claude for the answer is really only a difference in speed and detail. So what's the point? 

The point is that you are not meant to become a computer with perfect recollection and ability to manipulate data and information. You are meant to become a problem solver, to understand at some level how things work, how to look up what you don't know, and continuously evolve.
